Rising-power watch

Türkiye

Last updated: August 3, 2026 · Reviewed and improved in place, not re-published as a new post

Where things stand. Ankara moved fast to fill the vacuum left by Assad's fall, and the fastest-moving front is Syria. Erdoğan and Ahmed al-Sharaa met in February 2025 to discuss a defense pact and Turkish use of Homs-province air bases; by August 2025 their defense ministries had signed a training-and-equipment MOU covering Syria's rebuilt army — a real security relationship, though short of the formal basing rights still under separate negotiation. Turkish firms are positioned as lead players in Syria's reconstruction, forecast to exceed $20 billion in exports by 2028. At the same time, Turkey and Israel opened a genuine deconfliction channel over Syrian airspace from April 2025, modeled on existing Israel-Russia and Israel-US mechanisms — the Stimson Center's 2026 assessment reads this as managed rivalry, not resolution. Domestically, the PKK's May 2025 dissolution announcement, July 2025 weapons-burning ceremony, and October 2025 full withdrawal to northern Iraq closed a 40-year internal front, though analysts note this frees Ankara's hand against Kurdish forces in Syria and Iraq rather than signaling any Turkish pullback there.

The decision points

Four things are unresolved. First, whether the Syria relationship formalizes into actual Turkish basing rights or stays at the training-and-equipment level. Second, whether Israel-Turkey deconfliction holds as both states compete for influence in the same space, or breaks down under that competition. Third, whether the Zangezur Corridor — the long-discussed transport link through Armenia that would connect Turkey to Azerbaijan and the wider Turkic world — actually breaks ground; as of mid-2026 it remains unresolved, majority-US-owned (74%, versus Armenia's 26%), and behind the schedule Baku has repeatedly announced. Fourth, whether Erdoğan's coalition — which Al-Monitor reports holds enough seats to trigger an early election — moves toward a constitutional path for him to run again past his current term limit.

Who holds the leverage

Turkey's leverage is real and spans several domains at once: Baykar's drone exports hit $2.2 billion in 2025 (the Bayraktar TB2 alone sold to 36 countries), Turkey chairs an informal "Montreux Plus" regime that has restricted both Russian and NATO naval movement in the Black Sea since 2022, and Ankara hosted NATO's 2026 Leaders' Summit in July — only the second NATO summit Turkey has ever hosted. But two things cap that leverage rather than extend it. The economy is a constraint, not a springboard: the central bank held its policy rate at 37% through four straight 2026 meetings, the lira hit a record low near 47.5 to the dollar, and official annual inflation of 31.75% in July sits well below the 50.49% independent economists at ENAG calculate — a credibility gap that itself signals fragility. And Turkey's EU accession bid remains formally frozen, with the European Parliament's foreign affairs committee voting in April 2026 that talks "cannot resume" absent reversal of democratic backsliding — a judgment sharpened by the prosecution of Istanbul's opposition mayor, Ekrem İmamoğlu, across three simultaneous cases that Human Rights Watch has called politically motivated.

What to watch — the "Ottoman revenge" thesis, tested

The framing holds up cleanly in one domain: soft power, where the numbers are real and structural. Turkish TV drama exports passed $1 billion in 2025, reaching an estimated 1 billion viewers across roughly 170 countries, while the Diyanet religious authority runs a $3.7 billion budget and 644 staff abroad targeting 2.1 million people, concentrated in the Balkans, Central Asia, and Africa. It holds up more ambiguously in hard power — real gains in Syria, drone diplomacy, and Caucasus alliance-building sit alongside a widely repeated but independently unverified claim that Turkey fields "NATO's second-largest army," and a Zangezur corridor that is still more announcement than fact. It does not hold up in the economic and democratic dimensions: a central bank still fighting inflation, a currency at record lows, and a government spending political capital on prosecuting its most popular rival rather than consolidating a stronger claim to regional leadership. Read the thesis as directionally real in influence-projection — not yet backed by the fiscal or institutional foundations of an established regional power.
