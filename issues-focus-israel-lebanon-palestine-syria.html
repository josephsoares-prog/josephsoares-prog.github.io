Interlocking wars

Israel–Lebanon–Palestine–Syria

Last updated: August 3, 2026 · Reviewed and improved in place, not re-published as a new post

Where things stand. Four theaters, one zone. In Gaza, an October 2025 ceasefire ended the heaviest fighting, but its second phase — Hamas disarmament, a governance handover, and reconstruction — has stalled; hundreds have been killed since the ceasefire took hold, and Israel's cabinet approved an International Stabilization Force in late July, open only to states with normalized or peaceful ties to Israel. In Lebanon, a new war opened on March 2, days after the strike that killed Iran's Supreme Leader, and by June Israeli forces had pushed past the Litani River and occupied roughly a fifth of the country; Lebanon's currency has collapsed by nearly 98% and its economy has shrunk from $52 billion to about $18 billion. In Syria, the post-Assad government under Ahmed al-Sharaa is pursuing a security arrangement with Israel while still asserting Syria's claim to the Golan Heights.

The decision points

Three things are unresolved. First, whether Gaza's planned "National Committee for the Administration of Gaza" becomes a real governing body or stays, as one July analysis put it, something that "exists only in Cairo." Second, whether Israel's expanding footprint across Gaza, Lebanon, and Syria — described by one Carnegie Endowment panel as a "ring of buffer zones" spanning roughly 1,000 square kilometers — consolidates into a lasting security architecture or gets negotiated back. Third, whether Syria can secure a formal arrangement with Israel without conceding its Golan claim.

Who holds the leverage

Israel holds the clearest military leverage across all three fronts, including a buffer position in southern Lebanon reported to sit near Lebanon's most valuable untapped gas field. Lebanon's state has almost none: its IMF program remains stalled pending bank-restructuring legislation, and Hezbollah's own capacity has been degraded by what Israel says are hundreds of strikes since the 2024 truce. Syria's government is threading a narrow path — seeking sanctions relief (the US repealed the Caesar Act in December 2025) while avoiding the appearance of ceding ground on Golan or Lebanon. The US, as guarantor of both the Gaza ceasefire and Syria's sanctions relief, holds outsized leverage over how all three tracks move.

What to watch

Water is the underdiscussed layer beneath the visible conflict. Jordan draws on the Yarmouk basin under a 1956 allocation sharply reduced in practice, and the Golan Heights sit over aquifers that matter to Israel, Syria, and Jordan alike — but current reporting documents this far less rigorously than the territorial and political dimensions, so treat resource control as the connective thesis worth testing over time, not yet a fully documented fact. More immediately verifiable: whether Gaza's humanitarian numbers (roughly 70% of the population still lacking adequate shelter, per July reporting) keep improving, and whether Lebanon sees any real reconstruction financing before its currency crisis deepens further.
