Figures are preliminary results from Élections Québec: Parti Québécois 59, Quebec Liberal Party 40, Conservative Party of Quebec 19, Québec solidaire 9, Coalition Avenir Québec 0, out of 127 seats; 64 make a majority. Ninety to zero

Four years ago, one party won 90 of Quebec’s 125 seats. Last night, the same party won none. Not a few, none. And the party that replaced it does not have a majority. Here is what Quebec decided and what happens now.

I’m Joseph Soares. Before we begin, a word to you. If this episode is worth your time, like it, share it and subscribe, please.

The numbers

Let’s start with the numbers. Quebec’s National Assembly is the provincial legislature. It has 127 seats. This election cycle, you need 64 to govern alone. The party that wants Quebec to become a country won 59. Five short.

The Liberals, who want Quebec to stay in Canada, won 40, mostly around the island of Montreal. And in the Outaouais, the Conservatives won 19, mostly in the Quebec City region and the periphery. Four years ago, they had none.

So a party on the left that also supports independence won nine seats, and the Coalition Avenir Québec, the party that has governed since 2018, won 0. That is zero. None at all. Two voters out of three turned out, so just over 66%.

How it happened

Now look at how it happened. The governing party still took 13% of the vote, more than half a million people. That is more votes than Québec solidaire received. Yet Québec solidaire won nine seats. The government won none. The premier, Christine Fréchette, lost her own seat in Trois-Rivières by 499 votes.

I have been around politics for a long time. I’ve never seen a defeat like this one. No Quebec government has ever gone from power to zero seats in a single night. The closest thing I can think of is 1993 at the federal level, when Prime Minister Kim Campbell, at the time, lost her majority government and was left with only two seats on election night.

How a government wins nothing

Why does this happen? Well, because of how we vote in Quebec. In each riding, the candidate with the most votes wins the seat. Every other vote counts for, well, nothing. Finish second everywhere and you finish with nothing.

The arithmetic of a minority

So what happens now? Well, three things. First, let’s talk about arithmetic. The new premier, Paul St-Pierre Plamondon, needs five more votes to pass anything. He can find them on the left with Québec solidaire. He can find them on the right with the Conservatives. He can find them with the Liberals. The opposition can defeat him only if all three parties vote against him at the same moment. And those parties don’t have a lot in common. They don’t agree on very much.

That gives him manoeuvrable room. He can choose a different partner for every file.

The referendum question

Second, the referendum. Under Quebec law, a referendum question must be approved by the National Assembly. The Parti Québécois cannot do that alone. With Québec solidaire, it can. Together they hold 68 seats, but they won only about 40% of the vote. 40 is not 50 plus one.

And Mr. St-Pierre Plamondon has made a commitment: no referendum before January 2029, when the current American presidential term ends. The United States is Quebec’s largest trading partner. He has chosen stability in that relationship first. So for the next 27 months, the job is to govern. The last minority government his party formed lasted 19 months. That was Pauline Marois’ government.

The first budget

Third, the budget. It is the first vote this government cannot afford to lose. It’s a confidence vote. It means the defeat of the government and an automatic election. The party has promised a balanced budget within three years, lower immigration and a smaller health bureaucracy.

Watch who votes for that first budget. That is the real partner.

The Outaouais

One more thing close to home. I live in the Outaouais, just across the river from Ottawa. We have five seats here. Four of them went Liberal. One, Papineau, went to the Parti Québécois by 554 votes. So this region now has exactly one voice on the government side.

What I take from it

Here’s what I take from last night. A 90-seat majority feels permanent. It was gone in one term. Power is on loan. The people who lend it can take it all back at once, and they do not send a warning.

That is the subject of my book, Leadership Under Fire. It comes out on October 20th. Joseph Soares. Thank you for watching.
