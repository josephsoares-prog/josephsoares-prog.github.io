Continental watch

Africa

Last updated: August 3, 2026 · Reviewed and improved in place, not re-published as a new post

Where things stand. Africa is usually counted as 55 states by the African Union, which includes Western Sahara, or 54 by the UN, which does not — split along colonial-linguistic lines that still shape trade and diplomacy today: a Francophone bloc anchored by the CFA franc zone across West and Central Africa, a larger Anglophone bloc across East and Southern Africa, a five-country Lusophone bloc (Angola, Mozambique, Guinea-Bissau, Cape Verde, São Tomé and Príncipe), and a single Hispanophone enclave, Equatorial Guinea. These aren't just linguistic labels: the CFA franc is still pegged to the euro through a reserve arrangement with the French Treasury, and a 2019 plan to replace it with a new "Eco" currency remains unimplemented six years on.

The decision point

The sharpest current break isn't along the old colonial lines but a new one. Mali, Niger, and Burkina Faso — formerly ECOWAS's Francophone core — formed their own confederation in mid-2024, formally exited ECOWAS in January 2025, and by the end of 2025 had launched a joint 5,000-strong military force, a shared investment bank, and withdrawn from the ICC. Whether that bloc holds together, or fractures under the jihadist pressure it was partly built to resist, is one of the continent's live tests.

Who holds the leverage

Outside powers are competing directly for what's under African ground. The US signed a strategic minerals partnership with the Democratic Republic of Congo in December 2025, paired with a US-mediated DRC-Rwanda peace deal — the DRC holds more than 70% of the world's cobalt reserves. Guinea, Morocco, and Rwanda signed related cooperation agreements at a February 2026 US Critical Minerals Ministerial, though DRC's own lawyers have challenged the deal for bypassing parliamentary ratification. China's Africa engagement is reportedly leaning harder into critical minerals as Western attention fragments across other crises. Meanwhile in Mali specifically, the jihadist group JNIM has run a fuel and trade blockade on the capital since September 2025 — a reminder that in the Sahel, armed groups hold real leverage on the ground even as governments sign resource deals in the capitals.

What to watch

The demographic numbers are the biggest under-covered story on the continent. Africa's population passed 1.5 billion in 2024 and is projected to reach 2.5 billion by 2050 — 28% of the world's population, up from 10% in 1960 — with some 620 million people projected to enter the labor force over that period. The UN's own Economic Commission for Africa is explicit that "a demographic dividend is by no means guaranteed": roughly 10 million young Africans enter the labor market every year against only about 3 million new formal jobs, and more than 100 million employed young Africans already live in extreme working poverty. Whether this generation becomes the growth engine the raw numbers suggest, or a destabilizing pressure, depends far more on governance and job creation than on demographics alone.

Country watch: seven to track

Ivory Coast. Alassane Ouattara secured a fourth term in October 2025 with 89.77% of the vote after his two strongest rivals, Laurent Gbagbo and Tidjane Thiam, were disqualified on legal technicalities — a process that has left a legitimacy question hanging over an otherwise decisive win. The IMF projects 6.4% growth for 2026 on a cocoa-and-gold export mix (gold now edges cocoa as the top earner), and unlike its Sahel Alliance neighbors, Abidjan negotiated France's exit from its last military base in February 2025 while choosing to keep cooperating with Paris militarily — a moderate contrast to Mali, Niger, and Burkina Faso's harder break, even as jihadist spillover from that bloc remains Côte d'Ivoire's clearest security risk.

Nigeria. Bola Tinubu's government is managing a rare convergence: a US "Country of Particular Concern" designation over Christian-persecution claims, with Trump floating military action, alongside a security picture that has genuinely improved in the northeast (insurgent-held local government areas fell from 17 to under 6 since 2022) but remains fragile in the northwest, where banditry persists despite a 64% defense-budget increase since 2023. Growth is projected at 4.4% for 2026, helped by crude output hitting a 74-month high in June 2026 at 104% of Nigeria's OPEC quota.

DRC. The US-brokered June 2025 peace deal with Rwanda, paired with a December 2025 US-DRC minerals partnership, has not stopped the fighting — credible reporting puts civilian deaths at over 1,000 in the months since signing, and the US pact itself faces a constitutional challenge in Congolese courts for bypassing parliamentary approval. Minerals-driven growth is strong (one industry estimate puts 2026 growth near 7.8%, though this needs independent confirmation against IMF or World Bank figures), and DRC has moved from a cobalt export ban to a quota system while separately signing a new mining agreement with China in March 2026 — leaving Kinshasa courted by Washington and Beijing at once.

Angola. João Lourenço is barred from a third presidential term in 2027 but is seeking re-election as MPLA party president with no named successor, making the party's December 2026 congress arguably the real succession decision point. The economy is diversifying in earnest — non-oil FDI hit a record $959 million in 2025 and the Lobito Corridor rail project, linking Angola's port to DRC and Zambia's mineral belt, is central to a US strategy explicitly pitched as a counter to Chinese logistics dominance — even as Angola simultaneously seeks a new $4.8 billion China loan for a refinery while also reporting it is paying down existing Chinese debt, a contradiction still unresolved in the reporting.

Morocco. Western Sahara recognition keeps building: Washington reaffirmed its position on August 2, 2026, following a November 2025 UN Security Council resolution that boosted Morocco's autonomy plan and prompted Rabat to declare a "Unity Day" holiday. Domestically, PM Aziz Akhannouch announced he won't seek a third party term amid sustained "Gen Z 212" youth protests over public-service failures, while Morocco presses ahead with $32.5 billion in green hydrogen megaprojects backed by a genuinely multipolar investor mix of Gulf, Chinese, and European capital.

Egypt. Cairo secured a $1.8 billion IMF disbursement on July 31, 2026 — its seventh review, taking total program disbursements to roughly $7.3 billion — even as core inflation is projected to climb to 16.7% in the second half of the year and the IMF flags elevated public debt as an ongoing vulnerability. The more consequential dispute is regional: Egypt has called Ethiopia's completed Grand Renaissance Dam "illegal" and escalated to the UN Security Council with no binding Nile-sharing agreement in place, while separately fast-tracking a $25 billion nuclear plant with Russia — underscoring Egypt's habitual balancing between Western financing and Russian or Gulf partnerships.

Ethiopia. The Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam is complete and exporting power regionally, but with no Nile-sharing agreement and Egypt's dispute now at the UN Security Council. Domestically, Abiy Ahmed's Prosperity Party was projected to dominate June 2026 elections held under suspended polling in Tigray and parts of Amhara amid brief but real Ethiopia-TPLF clashes in January-February 2026, while a preliminary $1 billion Eurobond restructuring — a G20 Common Framework test case involving China, France, and the UK as creditors — remains only partially resolved after a prior deal collapsed.
