Quebec Votes: What to Watch Tonight

The question tonight is less who governs than how. A majority and a minority lead to two very different mandates, and one section of Quebec law explains why.

Quebec elects the 127 members of its National Assembly, the provincial legislature, today. Sixty-four make a majority.

Where the Race Stands

The final Léger poll, taken September 30 and October 1, puts the Parti Québécois (PQ, sovereigntist) at 29 percent, the Quebec Liberal Party (federalist) at 25 and the Conservative Party of Quebec at 20. Among French-speaking voters the PQ leads with 35 percent, ahead of the Conservatives at 19 [Léger].

The bigger number is another one: 77 percent of Quebecers say they want a change of government. The Coalition Avenir Québec (CAQ), in power since 2018, started the campaign near 24 percent and ends it at 14, according to Léger. The Qc125 model gives it between zero and two seats [Qc125].

Majority or Minority: The Real Question

Qc125 puts the PQ between 52 and 73 seats, on both sides of the 64-seat line, with a 38 percent chance of a majority. Another model, Poliwave, puts that chance at 49 percent. In plain terms, nobody knows.

The difference matters for a specific reason. Under Quebec’s Referendum Act, a referendum can only be held on a question approved by the National Assembly [Referendum Act, ss. 7–8]. A majority government can adopt one alone. A minority government needs another party’s votes. PQ leader Paul St-Pierre Plamondon has said he would push ahead with a referendum even without a majority [CTV]. The law would then require him to find an ally.

The precedent is recent. In 2012 Pauline Marois formed a PQ minority government with 54 of 125 seats. It was defeated nineteen months later [2012 election]. Constitutional scholar Patrick Taillon has also argued that even a PQ majority could not hold a referendum for two and a half to three years, given the consultation the party has promised.

Three Things to Watch

1. The Conservatives. The model’s range is 11 to 26 seats. Twelve gives a party official status in the Assembly, with the budget and speaking time that come with it. The party elected no members in 2022. Its leader, Éric Duhaime, is asking voters for the balance of power and opposes a third referendum. In a minority legislature, he could decide how long the government lasts.

2. The books. Three parties promise a return to balance, at different speeds. The PQ promises it in 2028–29, after a $6.5 billion deficit in 2026–27 [PQ framework]. The Liberals aim for 2029–30 [Noovo], as do the Conservatives, who propose $36.5 billion in spending reductions over five years. There is a PQ precedent: in the 1990s the government of Lucien Bouchard wrote the zero deficit into law [Budget 1998], at a time when sound finances were presented as a condition for a future referendum.

3. The Outaouais. The region around Gatineau, across the river from Ottawa, has five seats. The CAQ won four of them in 2022. Qc125 now puts the Liberals at 37 percent there, far ahead of the PQ at 18, and projects four or five Liberal seats [Qc125 Outaouais]. If the PQ forms the government, the region would sit almost entirely in opposition.

Two ridings deserve attention. In Papineau, the PQ’s Isabelle Millette trails the Liberal Isabelle Sabourin, 26 percent to 33, within the model’s margin [Qc125 Papineau]. In Hull, the Liberal candidate is Sacha Cannon, a lawyer born in the riding and the son of former federal minister Lawrence Cannon [PLQ]. The dominant regional issue remains health care: the Liberals promise pay parity with Ontario to keep health workers, at a cost of $90 to $98 million a year.

The Three Numbers Tonight

  • 64: whether the leading party has a majority.
  • 12: the Conservatives’ threshold.
  • Papineau: the most contested seat in the Outaouais.

We will be back tomorrow with what the result means.

Scope of evidence. Poll figures are from Léger’s release of October 3, 2026 (1,000 respondents, online, September 30 to October 1); the 14 percent figure for the CAQ was given by the pollster in an interview. Seat ranges and probabilities are from the Qc125 and Poliwave projections of October 3, 2026; a projection is an estimate, not a result. The referendum rule is from the Referendum Act (CQLR, c. C-64.1), sections 7 and 8. Patrick Taillon’s view was published by Droit-inc on September 10, 2026; Éric Duhaime’s position was reported by the Journal de Sherbrooke on October 2, 2026. Budget commitments are from the financial frameworks the parties published in September 2026; the precedent is from the Quebec budget speech of March 31, 1998. Candidates are named from party announcements and the Qc125 riding pages. This is an analytical description written before the polls closed; it endorses no party and no candidate.
