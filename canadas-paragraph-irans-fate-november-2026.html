Canada Didn't Need That Paragraph

On the Canada–UAE statement, three islands in the Strait of Hormuz, and what Ottawa should do next.

On October 28, Canada and the United Arab Emirates raised their relationship to a Strategic Partnership. Most of the joint statement reads exactly as you would expect. A trade agreement negotiated in forty-seven days and signed during the Crown Prince of Abu Dhabi's first official visit to Ottawa. A UAE pledge to facilitate up to $70 billion in investment into Canada. Cooperation on energy, artificial intelligence and critical minerals. For a government determined to reduce Canada's dependence on the United States, this playbook is working as designed, and diversification is a case I have made in these pages myself.

Then comes the last paragraph. In it, Canada and the UAE call for “an end to the occupation” of Greater Tunb, Lesser Tunb and Abu Musa, three small islands at the mouth of the Strait of Hormuz that Iran has held since 1971, when the Shah's navy took them as Britain withdrew from the Gulf. The United Arab Emirates was founded days later. It has claimed the islands ever since, and Iran has never entertained the claim. Tehran's foreign ministry fired back at Ottawa within seventy-two hours.

Canada did not need that paragraph. The trade agreement did not require it. The investment framework did not require it. Nothing in the economic package obliged Canada to adjudicate a fifty-five-year-old territorial dispute between two other countries, and Canadian governments of every stripe had declined to do exactly that for exactly that long.

The optics are terrible, and I say this as someone who wants the UAE partnership to succeed. The islands clause sits in a document about money, a page or two from a seventy-billion-dollar commitment. The regime's broadcasters are already telling Iranians that Canada's position came with a price tag. I do not believe Canada's word is for sale. But a signal is worth what its reader makes of it, and in Tehran this one is being read as a purchase.

Here is why that matters. The Islamic Republic lost its own people long ago, on the economy, on freedom, on basic dignity. The one card it has left is nationalism, the claim that only the regime stands between Iran and foreign powers who would carve the country apart. When a respected Western democracy declares Iranian-held territory to be someone else's, that card gets played free of charge. Consider one fact. Reza Pahlavi, the most prominent opponent the regime has, holds publicly that the islands are Iranian. When the regime's fiercest enemy and the regime agree on something, it is not a regime issue. It is a national one. Ottawa managed to offend the very Iranians it should be courting.

It is also done. The statement will not be unsigned, and a prolonged campaign against it would only feed the regime's favourite story, that Iranians abroad are at war with the West. So say it once, plainly, as I am doing here: the paragraph was a mistake. Then move on to the question that actually matters.

What should Canada do from here? Invest in the other Iran. If Ottawa has spent some of the Iranian people's goodwill on this statement, the least it can do is invest in their future. That means keeping the pressure on the regime and its Revolutionary Guards while drawing a bright line between Iran's rulers and Iran's people. It means consulting the nearly 280,000 Canadians of Iranian origin, one of the most accomplished communities in this country, before the next decision that touches their homeland. And it means engaging Reza Pahlavi and the democratic movement seriously, as the Leader of the Opposition has already begun to do, so that when change comes to Iran, Canada is standing where it has stood at its best moments.

That posture is not new for us. Canada refused for fifty years to recognize the Soviet annexation of the Baltic states, and was vindicated. Brian Mulroney led the Commonwealth against apartheid when London and Washington would not move. Canada was the first Western country to recognize Ukraine's independence in 1991, one day after the referendum. And in 2019, a Liberal government recognized the head of Venezuela's elected National Assembly as the country's legitimate interim leader rather than accept a stolen election. Both parties own this tradition. It has held up well every time.

Ninety million people live in Iran. They are young, educated, and finished with their rulers. A free Iran would be a partner for Canada on a scale no Gulf monarchy can offer. The UAE got its paragraph. The Iranian people should get Canada's hand.
