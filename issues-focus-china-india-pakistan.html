Nuclear triangle

China–India–Pakistan

Last updated: August 3, 2026 · Reviewed and improved in place, not re-published as a new post

Where things stand. China, India, and Pakistan are sometimes described together as holding two-thirds of the world's population. The more precise figure, worth stating plainly, comes in lower: China's population stood at 1.405 billion at the end of 2025, its fourth consecutive annual decline (China's National Bureau of Statistics, January 2026); India, which has not held a census since 2011, is estimated at roughly 1.477 billion under UN Population Division projections; Pakistan's 2023 census counted 241.5 million, with a 2026 UN-model estimate near 259 million. Combined, that's about 3.14 billion against a world population of roughly 8.3 billion — close to 38%. For context, China and India alone are just over a third of humanity, and all of Asia (roughly 59%) is the grouping that actually approaches two-thirds. Where these three countries' combined weight is unambiguous is the nuclear math, and there the gap is real and widening: SIPRI's 2025–2026 estimates put China's arsenal at roughly 600 warheads and growing faster than any other nuclear power (the Pentagon projects over 1,000 by 2030), while India's arsenal (about 190) overtook Pakistan's (about 170–180) for the first time in 2025 — a reversal of the historical South Asian pattern.

The decision points

Two things are live and unresolved. First, the Indus Waters Treaty, in force since 1960, has been in abeyance since India restricted its normal operation after the April 2025 Pahalgam attack, which killed 26 people in Indian-administered Kashmir. New Delhi has said flatly that "not a single drop" should reach Pakistan while it continues to "support terrorism"; Islamabad calls this "weaponization of water," and its defense minister has said the country would go to war over water security if conditions worsen. Chatham House's April 2026 assessment treats the treaty as at real risk of permanent collapse, with no bilateral re-engagement mechanism currently active. Second, an internal Pakistani crisis compounds the risk on the ground: protests in Azad Kashmir over reserved legislative seats turned violent from June 2026, killing dozens — including 22 Pakistani soldiers in a helicopter crash — destabilizing the Pakistani side of the Line of Control at the exact moment relations with India are frozen.

Who holds the leverage

China is running two relationships that pull in opposite directions, and has shown no sign it intends to choose between them. On one track, India and China spent 2025–2026 on a genuine, if fragile, thaw: full disengagement at the last Ladakh friction points by late 2024, a Modi-Xi meeting at the 2025 SCO summit, and border trade reopening on August 3, 2026 after a six-year suspension. On the other track, China has deepened military ties with Pakistan specifically — delivering J-10C fighters (Pakistan is China's only export customer for the jet) and offering up to 40 J-35 stealth aircraft, while Chinese state media has publicized claims that Pakistani-flown, Chinese-built jets downed Indian aircraft during the May 2025 clash, a claim India has not confirmed. Economically, the asymmetry cuts against the diplomatic thaw more than it supports it: India's growth (7.3% in 2025, per the IMF) is the fastest of the three, but its trade deficit with China widened to roughly $112 billion in the last fiscal year as China became India's top trading partner. Pakistan has neither India's growth rate (3.7% in the same year, missing its own target) nor China's independent economic weight, and leans on Chinese arms and financing to offset the gap.

What to watch

The nuclear trajectory is the more consequential trend line to track: China's declared "no first use" policy is officially unchanged but increasingly questioned by independent analysts given its expanding silo-based arsenal; India has paired its own declared no-first-use posture with explicit warnings against Pakistani "nuclear blackmail" since the 2025 crisis; and Pakistan remains the only one of the three with an explicit first-use doctrine, built around tactical battlefield weapons. None of that shifted this year. The population figure worth keeping in view alongside it is the precise one — roughly 38% of humanity, not two-thirds — attached to a widening, asymmetric nuclear balance.
